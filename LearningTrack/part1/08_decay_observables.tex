\begin{problem}{Problem 1 ★ — Derive two-body momentum and threshold scaling}
For $A\to B+C$, derive
$k=\sqrt{[M_A^2-(M_B+M_C)^2][M_A^2-(M_B-M_C)^2]}/(2M_A)$.
If the final relative partial wave is $\ell$, show why
$\Gamma\propto k^{2\ell+1}$ near threshold.

\textbf{Hint:} Work in the rest frame of $A$, with positive masses and
$M_A\ge M_B+M_C$. Use $\bm p_B=-\bm p_C$,
$E_i^2=M_i^2+k^2$, and $M_A=E_B+E_C$.
For the threshold law take a smooth short-range amplitude with a nonzero
leading coefficient: $\mathcal M_\ell\propto k^\ell$.
Two-body phase space contributes one factor of $k$; hold other smooth
factors finite. The power law is not a claim about long-range threshold effects.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
In the parent rest frame, energy conservation and on-shell conditions give the
K\"all\'en-function expression above. A partial-wave amplitude behaves as
$k^\ell$ at small $k$, while two-body phase space supplies one more power of
$k$, hence $\Gamma\propto k^{2\ell+1}$.
\end{solution}
\begin{quiz}
Why can a few-MeV mass error cause a large width error near threshold? \answer
the available momentum is small and enters with a positive odd power.
\end{quiz}

\begin{problem}{Problem 2 ★★ — Derive an electric-dipole radial rule}
For the E1 operator $\bm r$, use parity and angular momentum to show
$\Delta L=\pm1$ and no spin flip at leading order. Express the radial factor in
terms of reduced waves.

\textbf{Hint:} The leading E1 operator acts only on position and has spatial
rank 1 and odd parity. Use $|L_f-L_i|\le1\le L_f+L_i$ and opposite
orbital parities. With $\int|u_i|^2\dd r=\int|u_f|^2\dd r=1$,
separate the angular coefficient from the radial overlap; no numerical waves
are supplied.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$\bm r$ is a rank-1 odd-parity tensor, giving the triangle rule
$|L-L'|\le1$ and opposite orbital parity; thus $\Delta L=\pm1$. It does not act
on spin, so $\Delta S=0$. The radial matrix element is
$\int_0^\infty u_f(r)\,r\,u_i(r)\dd r$.
\end{solution}
\begin{quiz}
Can two solvers agree on masses but disagree on an E1 rate? \answer yes;
eigenvalues constrain the Hamiltonian globally, while the rate tests a weighted
wavefunction overlap.
\end{quiz}

\begin{problem}{Problem 3 ★★ — Explain origin sensitivity}
A normalized regular $S$ wave has $\Psi(\bm r)=R(r)Y_{00}$,
$Y_{00}=1/\sqrt{4\pi}$, and $u(r)=rR(r)$ with $u(0)=0$.
Assume a one-sided Taylor expansion through $r^2$ exists for $u$.
Show that $|\Psi(0)|^2=|R(0)|^2/(4\pi)$ and express $R(0)$ in terms of $u$.
If the first sampled radius is $h>0$, find the leading error in $u(h)/h$.
Which Taylor coefficient must vanish for an $O(h^2)$ error instead of $O(h)$?

\textbf{Hint:} Write $u(r)=ar+br^2+O(r^3)$ and divide by $r$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$Y_{00}=1/\sqrt{4\pi}$ and regularity gives $u(r)=R(0)r+O(r^2)$, so
$R(0)=u'(0)$. If values are known only at radii separated by $h$, then
$u(h)/h=R(0)+\tfrac12u''(0)h+O(h^2)$.
Thus the error is generally $O(h)$; it becomes $O(h^2)$ if $u''(0)=0$. One must study the limit as $h$ decreases, or
evaluate the derivative directly in a basis representation, before treating it
as an accurate value of $R(0)$.
\end{solution}
\begin{quiz}
Which is usually more demanding numerically: a mass or $|\Psi(0)|^2$? \answer
the origin density, because it depends on local derivative information.
\end{quiz}

\begin{problem}{Problem 4 ★★ — Propagate mixing into a decay amplitude}
Let $\ket i=\cos\theta\ket a+\sin\theta\ket b$, with orthonormal
$\ket a,\ket b$ and real $\theta$. For a fixed final channel define
$\mathcal M_a=\bra f T\ket a$ and $\mathcal M_b=\bra f T\ket b$.
Both amplitudes are evaluated at the physical initial mass and the same
final kinematics. Take $\Gamma_i=\Phi|\bra f T\ket i|^2$ with a common
phase-space factor $\Phi$.
Express $\Gamma_i$ in terms of the two complex amplitudes and identify the
interference term. Any unresolved quantum numbers are summed after squaring.

\textbf{Hint:} Apply linearity to the amplitude before taking its modulus squared.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$\mathcal M_i=\cos\theta\mathcal M_a+\sin\theta\mathcal M_b$, hence
\[
|\mathcal M_i|^2=\cos^2\theta|\mathcal M_a|^2+
\sin^2\theta|\mathcal M_b|^2+2\sin\theta\cos\theta
\operatorname{Re}(\mathcal M_a^*\mathcal M_b).
\]
Multiplying by the common factor $\Phi$ gives $\Gamma_i$.
Adding component widths would omit the last term.
\end{solution}
\begin{quiz}
Why does a basis-phase choice not change the interference physically? \answer
the corresponding component coefficient and matrix element change sign
together.
\end{quiz}

\begin{problem}{Problem 5 ★★★ — Separate spin algebra from wave distortion}
Assume $\Gamma_0/\Gamma_2=(15/4)|D_0/D_2|^2$ with $D_2\ne0$.
Here $D_J=\mathcal D[\psi_J]$ is the same linear radial functional applied
to the wave for spin $J$; all remaining factors are fixed, including the
short-distance spin-color factor $15/4$.
If the width ratio differs from $15/4$, what does that imply for
$|D_0|/|D_2|$? Design two comparisons that separate the fixed spin factor
from the effect of using different radial waves. State what is held fixed
and what is varied in each. No numerical $\mathcal D$ is needed.

\textbf{Hint:} Compare a common radial wave with separately calculated waves.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The deviation need not mean wrong spin algebra; it measures $J$-dependent wave
distortion when $D_0\ne D_2$. First evaluate both widths with a common central
wave, which should isolate $15/4$. Then use separately solved $J$ waves while
holding the short-distance coefficients fixed; the change isolates the radial
effect. This two-step comparison separates spin algebra from radial-wave effects
and is more informative than fitting one overall constant.
\end{solution}
\begin{quiz}
What does a decay calculation validate that a spectrum fit cannot? \answer wave
normalization, local/overlap structure, mixing phases, and operator selection
rules.
\end{quiz}

\begin{problem}{Problem 6 ★★ — Turn meson sizes into an effective decay $\beta$}
For the Appendix-B oscillator convention
$\psi_{nL}\propto e^{-\beta^2r^2/2}$, use the diagonal matrix elements from
Sheet 3, Problem 6 to derive $\expect{r^2}$ and $\expect{p^2}$. Invert each
relation to define an effective $\beta_r$ and $\beta_p$ for a realistic
wavefunction. Evaluate the $1S$ rms separation and rms momentum at
$\beta=0.40\,\mathrm{GeV}$. Why need $\beta_r$ and $\beta_p$ not agree, and why
is this decay-model $\beta$ distinct from the variational oscillator-basis
scale used to solve the Hamiltonian?

\textbf{Hint:} The oscillator radial index here starts at $n=0$, so the
spectroscopic $1S$ state has $n=L=0$. Use the full Laguerre oscillator
functions of Sheet 3; the displayed Gaussian is only their envelope.
Report $r_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{\expect{r^2}}$ in both $\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}$ and
fm, with $\hbar c=0.19733\,\mathrm{GeV\,fm}$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Writing $C_{nL}=2n+L+\tfrac32$, the oscillator moments are
\[
 \expect{r^2}_{\rm SHO}=\frac{C_{nL}}{\beta^2},\qquad
 \expect{p^2}_{\rm SHO}=C_{nL}\beta^2 .
\]
Thus a realistic wave defines two generally different one-moment fits,
\[
 \beta_r=\sqrt{\frac{C_{nL}}{\expect{r^2}}},\qquad
 \beta_p=\sqrt{\frac{\expect{p^2}}{C_{nL}}}.
\]
For $1S$, $C_{00}=3/2$. At $\beta=0.40\,\mathrm{GeV}$,
\[
 r_{\rm rms}=\frac{\sqrt{3/2}}{0.40}=3.06\,\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}
              \simeq0.604\,\mathrm{fm},\qquad
 p_{\rm rms}=0.40\sqrt{3/2}=0.490\,\mathrm{GeV},
\]
where $\hbar c=0.19733\,\mathrm{GeV\,fm}$. Equality $\beta_r=\beta_p$ means that the two moments satisfy
$\expect{r^2}\expect{p^2}=C_{nL}^2$. The corresponding SHO state satisfies
this relation; for general excited states, matching two moments alone does not
establish the full wave shape. Disagreement means that one oscillator scale
cannot reproduce both moments. In the paper, $0.40\,\mathrm{GeV}$ is a common physical scale for the
surrogate SU(6)/SHO decay wavefunctions. By contrast, the solver's basis
$\beta$ is a numerical representation parameter whose effect must disappear
as the basis converges.
\end{solution}
\begin{quiz}
May a native-wave decay calculation refit a new SHO $\beta$ without changing
its model? \answer no. Once the calculated wavefunction is used directly, a
second physical size parameter would replace or double-model its spatial
information; a fitted SHO $\beta$ belongs only to an explicit surrogate-wave
approximation.
\end{quiz}
